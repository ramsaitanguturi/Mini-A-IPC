\section{Concurrency, Adaptive Evaluation, and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section evaluates multi-process concurrency scaling, analyzes adaptive selector performance and latency regret, interprets custom kernel mailbox findings, presents systems hypotheses and practitioner recommendations, and synthesizes answers to the five core research questions (RQ1--RQ5).

\subsection{Multi-Producer Multi-Consumer (MPMC) Scalability}

Real-world systems frequently deploy concurrent multi-process topologies. The Mini-A-IPC concurrency benchmark (\texttt{src/concurrency\_benchmark.c}) evaluates five concurrency topologies: 1P:1C (baseline), 2P:1C (producer contention), 4P:1C (high producer contention), 1P:2C (work stealing), and 4P:4C (symmetric MPMC). Fig.~\ref{fig:concurrency} illustrates throughput scaling across these topologies for supported mechanisms.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.74\columnwidth]{figures/concurrency_scalability.pdf}
\caption{Multi-Producer Multi-Consumer (MPMC) Concurrency Throughput for 256-Byte Payloads across Five Process Topologies.}
\label{fig:concurrency}
\end{figure}

Our empirical findings and architectural analysis establish a fundamental distinction between operating system capabilities and application-level transport design:

\textbf{1) Implementation Constraints vs. OS Semantics:}
At the operating system level, Linux IPC primitives offer diverse concurrency capabilities. Linux pipes and FIFOs guarantee atomic writes up to \texttt{PIPE\_BUF} (4,096~bytes) for concurrent writers into a single consumer, but multiple uncoordinated consumers reading from a shared stream risk partial-read fragmentation without application framing. Unix Domain stream sockets support multi-client topologies via dedicated connection descriptors, but a single stream pair is point-to-point. Shared memory supports MPMC communication when paired with lock-free concurrent ring buffers; however, Mini-A-IPC's shared mailbox (\texttt{src/ipc\_shm.c}) is intentionally implemented as an alternating single-slot SPSC ping-pong design with POSIX semaphores.
Consequently, Mini-A-IPC's concurrency harness marks point-to-point stream pairs and the single-slot SPSC mailbox as \texttt{UNSUPPORTED\_BY\_DESIGN} ($rc = 3$) for M:N topologies to prevent data corruption.

\textbf{2) Native MPMC Transports:} In contrast, kernel-managed queues and connection-multiplexed transports scale robustly across multi-process topologies:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=1pt]
    \item \textit{Unix Domain Sockets} sustained \textbf{1,737,338.0~msg/s} ($444.76$~MB/s at 256~B; $424.15$~MiB/s) at 4P:4C via full-duplex socket buffer multiplexing.
    \item \textit{System V Message Queues} sustained \textbf{896,203.2~msg/s} ($229.43$~MB/s at 256~B; $218.80$~MiB/s) via kernel atomic queue locking.
    \item \textit{POSIX Message Queues} processed \textbf{429,577.2~msg/s} ($109.97$~MB/s; $104.88$~MiB/s) cleanly at 4P:4C.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[!t]

\caption{Intra-Trial Latency Regret (\%) of Static Size-Threshold Selector vs. Empirical Global Oracle Across Seven Payload Classes ($N=7$ Randomized Trials).}

The evaluation highlights four core insights into adaptive systems engineering:
\textbf{1) Static Selector Accuracy:} Evaluated against the 6-mechanism space, the static selector matches the global oracle for 1 of 7 classes (\textbf{14.29\% global accuracy}). Evaluated strictly between candidate transports (\texttt{shm} vs. \texttt{pipe}), it correctly selects the faster candidate for 4 of 7 classes (\textbf{57.14\% candidate accuracy}), routing all payloads $>1$~KB to Pipes where shared memory is bounded, while semaphore contention elevates shared memory latency above pipes for $\le 1$~KB.
\textbf{2) Intra-Trial Regret:} Across matched trial blocks, mean latency regret is \textbf{27.68\%}. At 16~KB, the selector routes to Anonymous Pipes (oracle). Matched baseline pipe and adaptive runs ($N=7$) yielded an empirical difference of $-3.27\%$ ($136.00\,\mu\text{s}$ vs. $140.60\,\mu\text{s}$) due to within-block jitter, statistically indistinguishable from zero ($t = -0.189, p = 0.86, \text{df} = 6$; Wilcoxon $W = 11.0, p = 0.69$). At 256~KB, selecting Pipes ($957.62\,\mu\text{s}$) over Unix Sockets ($449.64\,\mu\text{s}$) yields $112.97\%$ regret due to pipe buffer exhaustion. In mixed 80/20 workloads, the selector incurs $79.43\,\mu\text{s}$ vs. $57.21\,\mu\text{s}$ for Pipes from alternating dispatch overhead.
\textbf{3) Negligible Overhead:} Over 1,000,000 iterations in compiled C, the static selector requires only \textbf{0.66~ns per call} ($< 0.0005\%$ of round-trip time).
\textbf{4) Offline 3-Tier Calibration vs. Online Control:} Calibrating an offline policy across three regimes—(i)~Sub-KB ($\le 256$~B) routing to System V MQ (64~B, 256~B); (ii)~Intermediate transfers (1~KB--16~KB) dispatching to empirical crossover winners: 1~KB to Unix Sockets ($128.28\,\mu\text{s}$), 4~KB to Named FIFOs ($119.44\,\mu\text{s}$, matching \texttt{PIPE\_BUF}), and 16~KB to Anonymous Pipes ($140.60\,\mu\text{s}$); and (iii)~Large transfers ($\ge 64$~KB) routing to Unix Sockets (64~KB, 256~KB) via buffer auto-tuning—matches 100\% (7 of 7 decisions) of oracle choices across the test suite. This in-sample baseline establishes achievable bounds rather than online generalization. While EWMA telemetry and hysteresis are implemented in \texttt{src/selector.c}, benchmarks exercised deterministic thresholding; closed-loop online adaptation remains future work.

\subsection{Kernel Mailbox Evaluation and the Scheduler Floor}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.35\columnwidth]{figures/kernel_vs_userspace.pdf}
\caption{Custom Linux Kernel Mailbox (\texttt{/dev/aipc}) In-Process Loopback Latency Floor vs. KVM Multi-Process Scheduling Floor (64 B Payloads).}
\label{fig:kernel_floor}
\end{figure}

Measurements of \texttt{/dev/aipc} originate from native driver validation (Fedora 45, Linux 7.3.3, Intel Core 7 240H, 350,000 loopback exchanges). In single-process loopback, the mailbox achieved an uncontended floor of $0.72\text{--}0.79\,\mu\text{s}$ median latency and $2.95\text{--}11.45\,\mu\text{s}$ across-trial mean RTT across evaluated payloads ($2.95\text{--}3.35\,\mu\text{s}$ for $\le 1$~KB; Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_results}, Fig.~\ref{fig:kernel_floor}). On our primary KVM testbed, module loading was recorded as \texttt{BLOCKED} due to unprivileged guest boundaries; multi-process transfers over \texttt{/dev/aipc} were not directly measured. In multi-process configurations, communication is bounded by the observed \textbf{95--130~$\mu$s} KVM context-switch floor; driver optimizations cannot bypass process descheduling, VM exits, and hypervisor dispatch delays.

\subsection{Buffer Saturation and Context-Switch Dynamics}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.32\columnwidth]{figures/context_switches.pdf}
\caption{Voluntary Context Switches vs. Payload Size for Stream Transports, demonstrating buffer exhaustion for 64 KB pipe buffers at 256 KB transfers.}
\label{fig:context_switches}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:context_switches} exposes the cause of pipe throughput collapse at 256~KB. Up to 64~KB, voluntary context switches remain flat at $\approx 20,800$ per trial block across stream transports. At 256~KB, transfers exceed the 64~KB pipe buffer capacity; the sender blocks upon filling the buffer, forcing four chunked transfers and an explosive \textbf{7.0$\times$} surge in voluntary context switches (to 146,374). In contrast, Unix Domain Sockets leverage socket buffer auto-tuning (\texttt{tcp\_wmem}/\texttt{rmem}), capping switches at 62,400 and sustaining 1,304.44~MB/s wire bandwidth (652.22~MB/s payload; 1,244.01~MiB/s wire).

\subsection{Synthesis and Answers to Research Questions}
\textbf{RQ1 (Payload Sensitivity):} Hierarchy depends on payload: sub-512~B gives a descriptive lead to System V MQ ($123.33\,\mu\text{s}$ at 64~B, $115.13\,\mu\text{s}$ at 256~B; $p \ge 0.05$); 1~KB and 64~KB favor Unix Sockets ($128.28\,\mu\text{s}$, $123.53\,\mu\text{s}$); 4~KB favors Named FIFOs ($119.44\,\mu\text{s}$); 16~KB favors Anonymous Pipes ($140.60\,\mu\text{s}$); and 256~KB favors Unix Sockets ($449.64\,\mu\text{s}$, $2.54\times$ faster than pipes, $p < 0.001$).
\textbf{RQ2 (Memory vs. Stream):} POSIX SHM eliminates data copies (0.00 syscalls) and cuts syscalls by 48.5\% (2.06 futex vs. 4.00 for streams).
\textbf{RQ3 (Adaptive Selection):} Static selector evaluates in 0.66~ns with 27.68\% mean regret (57.14\% candidate accuracy; 14.29\% global accuracy; EWMA feedback architected).
\textbf{RQ4 (Kernel Mailbox):} A character device achieves a $0.72\,\mu\text{s}$ loopback floor ($2.95\text{--}11.45\,\mu\text{s}$ across-trial mean RTT) in native validation, but multi-process latency is bounded by the 95--130~$\mu\text{s}$ KVM scheduling floor.
\textbf{RQ5 (Concurrency):} Unframed streams cannot support M:N concurrency; Unix Sockets sustain 1.74M msg/s and System V MQ sustains 896k msg/s at 4P:4C.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\textbf{Internal Validity:} $N = 7$ randomized blocks (70,000 exchanges/cell) yield narrow 95\% CIs ($\le \pm 0.925 \cdot s$). Paired $t$-tests confirm large-payload socket superiority at 256~KB ($t=6.40, p < 0.001$) is statistically significant; sub-kilobyte variations ($p \ge 0.05$) reflect descriptive rankings within KVM noise.
\textbf{Construct \& External Validity:} While architectural boundaries (\texttt{PIPE\_BUF} 4,096~B atomicity, 64~KB pipe buffer exhaustion) reflect invariant Linux mechanisms, empirical latency rankings reflect our tested KVM guest (Fedora 45, kernel 7.3.3); on bare metal, the 95--130~$\mu$s floor may contract. Full logs ensure reproducibility.
